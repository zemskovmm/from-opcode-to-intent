# B11 AI coding evidence update

Supporting data. Attached to 11 The bottleneck moved.

Keep the METR results together as one dated study sequence. The early-2025 randomized study covered 246 real tasks from 16 experienced open-source developers in familiar repositories; AI-allowed work took 19% longer in that setting.[1] The February 2026 update says newer tools were likely more helpful, but selection effects, changed compensation, and concurrent-agent time measurement make its raw estimates unreliable for sizing current uplift.[2]

This is a randomized study followed by an attempted continuation with an explicitly unreliable effect-size estimate—not a timeless verdict about AI coding. Do not use the 2026 signal to erase the 2025 result, or the 2025 result to describe later tools.

Positive randomized field evidence also exists. Microsoft reports three ordinary-course-of-business experiments across 4,867 developers, with a combined estimate of 26.08% more completed tasks after random access to an AI code-completion assistant; the individual experiments were noisy.[3]

The METR outcome is time per pre-specified issue in a small, experienced-maintainer sample. Microsoft reports completed-task throughput in different organizations and populations. The percentages are not interchangeable, so neither result cancels the other. Both support a narrower claim: measure end-to-end accepted changes, regressions, review/rework, and developer experience by task class rather than treating generated code or a single clock as product value.[1][3]

Evidence labels matter: a randomized study can support a causal claim only in its studied setting; a benchmark probes a constructed task; and a vendor engineering report is a case study, not an independent productivity result. None of this evidence evaluates IDD itself.
